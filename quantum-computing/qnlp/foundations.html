Logical Natural Language Processing

Origins

Logical approaches to NLP stem from efforts to formalise representations of natural langauge, such as those proposed by R. Montague, 1970.

Early attempts to produce a logical language model preceed the notion of NLP itself. C. S. Pierce, 1887 is perhaps the earliest example of such thinking. Pierce likened a sentence to a molecule, with each word forming bonds:

C. S. Pierce's original diagram relating the proposition "John gives John to John" to ammonia (p. 169)

Dependent on its grammatical role within the sentence each word is given a number of bonds. These bonds each have a type (for example, 'noun'), and are capable of binding with other words possessing the appropriate receiving type. In this model a complete, grammatically correct sentence is one in which no loose bonds are present (that is, every bond has formed a connection.)

Pregroups

J. Lambek, 2006's work in formalising such concepts through the introduction of 'pregroups' has been pivotal in the development of logically based QNLP. Pregroups are a specialisation of monoids for the purpose of language processing. Ordering is introduced through the inclusion of left and right adjoints which, when ordered correctly, produce the identity element through the group's binary operation. Each word is assigned pregroups depending on its role within the sentence, with a complete sentence reducing to a single sentence type.

To explain the concept further, take the simple subject-verb-object sentence 'Mice eat cheese'. Let s, v, o represent the subject, verb and object types respectively, and let a fourth type, Ω represent a complete sentence. Each of these types then also has an adjoint possessing a direction, either left, l, or right, r. On the generic type g these adjoints act such that glg→I→ggl and ggr→I→grg.

Assigning the pregroups Mice→s, eat→srΩol, cheese→o we may observe the process of reduction reveal a valid sentence:

s(srΩol)o→(ssr)Ω(olo)→ IΩI→Ω

Though this example is trivial, it is revealing of the fundamental logic underlying this approach to understanding language.

String diagrams

String diagrams are often used to represent grammatical structures based on pregroup logic. They are formal, graph-algebraic structures consisting of boxes, which contain words, and strings/wires, which connect boxes. Wires are enriched with grammatical types to allow restrict their connectivity in line with the structure of pregroups. The shape a wire takes also conveys meaning. An upwardly concave wire is known as a cup, and represents a contraction within the diagram.

String diagram for 'Mice eat cheese'. A single dangling wire representing the sentence type, Ω, represents a grammatically correct sentence.

By representing pregroups through string diagrams their structure becomes more clear. The verb 'eat' can be thought of as expecting a subject to its left, and an object to its right. These requirements are fulfilled by 'Mice' and 'cheese' respectively, creating cups that prevent dangling wires.

Structures like these represent a sophisticated approach to natural language processing compared to more basic methods, such as bag-of-words or stairs models. In the next section the implementation of these structures will be introduced.

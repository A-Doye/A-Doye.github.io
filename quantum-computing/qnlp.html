Quantum Natural Language Processing

Computers are unimaginative creatures, rigourously following commands given to them by programs. These programs are usually created using programming languages, languages specifically created for computers to understand. Usually these langauges have a small vocabulary and are unambiguous.

Given the amount of knowledge stored in every day language, it is advantageous that we develop methods for computers to understand everyday language. This field is known as natural language processing (NLP).

Introducing Quantumness

Natural language processing is usually aproached as a machine learning task. Given a corpus of examples of langauge, how can we construct an algorithm such that the computer is able to extract meaninguful information from further examples of the language?

Quantum computers are considered by many to possess the potential to improve machine learning capabilities over their classical counterparts. This thought is well founded. For example, given that in the general case a larger corpus to learn from improves results, the ability to search such a large space more efficiently (through quantum algorithms such as Grover's search) would suggest the potential for more effective models.

Quantum natural language processessing (QNLP) explores this hypothesis in the context of NLP. Typically algorithms within the field follow one of two approaches:

  1. Statistical: Basing understanding on trends within the corpus, following the oft quoted principle 'you will know a word by the neighbours it keeps'. Most classical models also follow this aproach.
  2. Logical: Basing understanding on grammar. The grammatical role of a word within a sentence dictates how it is understood.

The latter of these approaches is a popular area of research within QNLP. A grammatical apporach possesses the advantage of being more easily understood by humans when trying to follow the devices' logic. This stands in contrast to statistical methods which often rely on neural network structures for learning. Such structures are effective, but pose a black box to humans which wish to follow how a computer came to the result that it did.
